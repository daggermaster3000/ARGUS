The junction pipeline, explained
    An interactive explainer

    From a slide scan to a count of migrating cells

    How the pipeline finds where two fused organoids meet, marks a band of tissue just past that junction, finds every cell in the band and decides which cells carry the label. Every figure below is live: you can drag, click and play it.

    0

    The question

    An assembloid is two organoids grown together. One carries cells labelled with AF488; the other carries its own label, AF555. Some labelled cells leave the organoid they started in and migrate into the other one. How many do so, how far do they go, and does this differ between genotypes (WT3 against GD1)?

    By eye, a person answers this on one section in three steps: find where the two organoids meet (the junction), look just past it into the other organoid, and count the bright cells there. The dataset has about 1,450 sections, so the computer does the same steps in the same order. This page shows each step on two real sections from the same slide. On both, a person also drew the junction by hand. On one section the automatic junction finder worked: its line lies close to the hand-drawn one. On the other it failed: its line lies far from the hand-drawn one, yet no warning was raised. Most figures have a Sample switch: use it to change between the two sections and see where they part ways.

    1

    Pre-screen: is this section usable at all?

    Before any analysis, a person looked at every section once and sorted it into classes (the OASIS pre-screen). The pipeline assumes two fused organoids in one clean piece. A section that does not look like that should not reach the statistics just because the program can draw a line through it.

    ClassValuesWhat it says
    Passyes / noUsable at all.
    ShapeFused pair · Damaged · Separated · Empty/debrisTwo organoids that touch; fused but with a broken outline; two that never touched; no organoid.
    Rippedyes / noA tear runs through the tissue.
    Foldedyes / noPart of the section lies on itself (double brightness).
    Qualitye.g. focus, stainingWhether single nuclei can be told apart.

    What goes on to the analysis: every section with Pass = yes, except those with Shape = Separated (they have no junction to find). Damaged sections are analysed too, but their junction is not taken from the program: it is drawn by hand (section 10).

    What can go wrong

    • The pre-screen is a quick look at a whole section. All 30 validation samples passed it, yet the junction finder failed on 8 of them. Passing the pre-screen does not mean the junction will be easy to find.
    • A tear or a fold can be missed on a small picture. It then turns up later as a failure of the junction finder (section 10).
    2

    The overview: a small picture of the whole section

    A slide scan has tens of megapixels and takes about a gigabyte. An organoid is millimetres across, and detail finer than a few micrometres says nothing about where the junction is. So the junction is found on a shrunken copy of the scan, the overview, at about 5 µm per pixel. That is why finding it takes only seconds.

    • Flatten the depth. Each pixel takes its brightest value over the z-planes (a maximum projection).
    • Shrink. Each square block of full-resolution pixels becomes one overview pixel. For the label channels (AF488, AF555), the overview pixel takes the value of one pixel of the block (keep one pixel), not the mean. A labelled cell a few pixels wide then keeps its full brightness instead of being averaged into the dark background around it.
    • For the nuclear stain, a second copy takes the mean of the block. Averaging blurs single nuclei into an even grey, so the faint, continuous dark line where the two organoids meet (a thin strip with fewer nuclei) stands out. Section 6 works on this copy.

    The figure shows a real crop at full resolution. Move the slider to set the size of an overview pixel: each square block of that size becomes one pixel. Switch between mean of the block and keep one pixel and watch what happens to a bright cell.

    What can go wrong

    • The overview is flat, so the junction is drawn where it appears in the flattened picture. A junction that tilts through the thickness of the section shows up as a blurred band, not a sharp line.
    • A dark line thinner than an overview pixel can vanish when the block is averaged. In these sections the dark line is about 20 µm wide, which is four overview pixels.
    3

    Where is the tissue?

    Every later step needs to know which pixels are tissue. The nuclear stain tells us: tissue is where the nuclei are. But single nuclei are dots with gaps between them. So the stain is first smoothed (each pixel becomes a weighted mean of its neighbours, over about 10 µm), then thresholded (pixels above a set value count as tissue), then tidied. One organoid of a pair is often faint in the nuclear stain. So every other channel is thresholded in the same way, and a pixel counts as tissue if it is brighter than the bare slide in any channel. Without this, the faint organoid would be left out, and the junction would stop short of the tissue's outline.

    a · Smoothing merges the nuclei into one tissue signal

    The nuclear stain of the overview (5 µm per pixel). Choose how much to smooth. Without smoothing, the gaps between nuclei would count as background. With too much smoothing, the dark line between the organoids blurs into its surroundings. The pipeline uses 10 µm.

    b · The threshold: not halfway, but a fifth of the way

    The histogram counts pixels by their smoothed value. It has a hump of background (left) and a hump of tissue (right). Otsu's method, a standard rule, finds the value that splits the two humps best. The pipeline does not set its threshold there: one organoid of a pair is often dimmer than the other, and a threshold at the midpoint would drop it. Instead, it takes the median of each hump and sets the threshold a fifth of the way from the background median to the tissue median. Each of the other channels gets its own threshold in the same way (step 2 of part c). Drag the handle on the histogram and watch the mask of the nuclear stain on the right: pixels above the threshold are tissue (outlined), the washed-out ones are background.

    c · Tidy the mask, one operation at a time

    Opening removes specks and one-pixel spurs. Closing fills gaps and notches narrower than about 15 µm. Then every connected piece smaller than a tenth of the largest piece is dropped as debris. (A second organoid lying apart would be about as big as the first, so it is kept.) Finally the holes are filled. The junction is searched for inside the filled mask; the mask with its holes is used for measuring. Step through the operations with the player.

    What can go wrong

    • Stitched scans fill the area outside the imaged tiles with exact zeros. Those pixels are left out of every step, so this padding is never mistaken for background.
    • Debris bigger than a tenth of the largest piece is kept as tissue.
    • A channel that also lights up the slide around the tissue (mounting medium, a smear) makes the outline grow onto that area. For such sections, turn off Find the tissue in every channel in the viewer's panel.
    4

    Where are the labelled cells?

    The raw AF488 channel is not a map of cells. It also shows autofluorescence, folds and thick parts of the section, which are bright but spread out (a haze). A cell, by contrast, is a small spot brighter than its surroundings. Three steps keep the spots and drop the haze.

    • Take away the local median. From each pixel, subtract the median of the 45 µm square around it; values below zero become zero. A cell covers only a few pixels of the square, so the median ignores it and gives the background level around it. A haze is as bright as its neighbours, so it drops to zero.
    • Scale by the brightest half-percent of pixels, so the map does not depend on how bright the channel is overall.
    • Smooth over 100 µm into a density (how much labelled signal there is per area). Divide by the share of tissue under the smoothing window, so the edge of the section does not read as "fewer cells".

    a · What the median does, along one line of pixels

    A made-up brightness profile along one row of pixels: the sharp peaks are cells, the broad bump is a haze. Teal fill: what is left after the local median is taken away. Change the window with the slider, or untick the box to see the profile unchanged.

    b · On the sections

    What can go wrong

    • Labelled cells on both sides. If cells have spread widely, or the label is everywhere, the density cannot tell which organoid is which. The program then raises the warning labelled cells on both sides. It is the most common warning in this dataset: it was raised on 12 good lines and 3 bad ones, so on its own it says little.
    • Scaling would stretch a channel with no cells at all into a map that looks as full as a real one. So each channel is checked first: its brightest points must stand out by at least 6 robust SDs (a spread measure that ignores a few extreme values; see the glossary) above the channel's own background texture.
    5

    One seed in each organoid

    The density tells which end of the section is which organoid, but it is too uneven to show where the border runs. So it is used only to place a seed in each organoid: a patch of tissue that surely belongs to that organoid. The junction is then searched for between the two seeds.

    • Labelled core: the largest patch where the AF488 density is above half its range.
    • Other core: found the same way from the other organoid's own label (AF555), where AF555 outweighs AF488 and outside the labelled core. It is used if it covers at least 2 % of the tissue.
    • Pull both back: of each core, keep only the half furthest from the other core. These halves are the labelled seed (teal) and the other seed (coral). Each now starts well inside its own organoid. Distances are measured through the tissue, not straight across a notch in the outline.

    Step through the masks with the player. The coloured map underneath is the density the current step works from.

    What can go wrong

    • Without a usable AF555 core, the other seed is the tissue with little AF488 that lies furthest from the labelled seed. A small seed at the far edge leaves the junction more room to wander than a large seed does. On about a third of sections, the junction moved by more than 100 µm when the rule for this second seed was changed.
    • A seed in the wrong organoid puts the whole junction in the wrong place.
    6

    The price of cutting each pixel

    Where two organoids have fused, the nuclear stain shows a thin dark line: a strip with fewer nuclei. The program splits the tissue into two halves, one around each seed; the border between the halves is called the cut. To make the cut run along the dark line, every pixel gets a price, a number between 0 and 1 for how costly it is to cut through that pixel: low on the dark line, 1 in plain tissue. The program then looks for the cut with the lowest total price (sections 7 and 8).

    • fine = the nuclear stain (block mean, section 2) smoothed over 8 µm. This merges single nuclei but keeps the dark line.
    • coarse = the same stain smoothed over 60 µm: the general level of the surroundings.
    • ratio = fine ÷ coarse, kept between 0.3 and 1. It is about 1 in the middle of either organoid, whether that organoid is bright or dim, so brightness differences between the organoids do not matter. Anything brighter than its surroundings stays at 1.
    • price = ratio6. Raising the ratio to the power 6 makes a small dip count: a line 20 % darker than its surroundings costs about a quarter as much to cut as plain tissue.

    Left: the price map (dark = cheap to cut). Right: price against ratio for each power; move the mouse over the map to see where a pixel falls on the curve. Use Power to compare other powers.

    What can go wrong

    • A crack, a fold or a tear is a dark line too. A tear is empty, so it is the darkest and therefore the cheapest thing in the section. Holes and the outer edge of the section are also cheap. That only matters where the cut can follow them to separate the two seeds (sections 7 and 8).
    7

    The minimum cut

    Think of the tissue as a net: every pixel is a knot, tied to each of its neighbours by a join. Cutting a join costs the mean price of its two pixels. The minimum cut is the cheapest set of joins to cut so that the two seeds end up in separate pieces of the net. The border between the organoids is drawn along those joins.

    How the computer finds it. It does not try every possible cut, and it does not flood the tissue (the floods in part b are only an illustration). Instead it treats every join as a pipe whose width is the join's price, and asks how much water can flow from one seed to the other:

    1. Push water. Find a route of pipes from the labelled seed to the other seed in which every pipe still has room. Send as much water along it as the narrowest pipe on the route can still take. That pipe is now full.
    2. Repeat until no route with room left remains. The flow is then blocked by a set of full pipes somewhere between the seeds: the bottleneck.
    3. Read off the cut. Mark every pixel that water could still reach from the labelled seed through pipes that are not full. These pixels form the labelled side; all other pixels form the other side. Every join between the two sides is full, so these joins are the bottleneck. Their total width equals all the water that got through (the max-flow / min-cut theorem), so no cheaper cut exists. These joins are the cut; sections 8 and 9 turn it into a smooth line.

    Because a pipe's width is its price, the narrow pipes (on dark pixels) fill first, and the bottleneck forms where they are. So:

    • If every pixel has the same price, the cheapest cut is simply the shortest way across the tissue: it finds the narrowest neck between two lobes.
    • With the price from the stain, running along a dark line costs less than crossing plain tissue, so the cut bends onto the line, even if that makes it longer.
    • The price is added up over the whole cut. A single dark speck saves the price of one pixel; a continuous dark line saves it along its whole length. That is why the cut follows lines and ignores specks.

    b · On the section: a picture of why the cut lands on the dark line

    On a real section there are far too many pushes to show one by one, so this figure gives an illustration instead. Imagine pouring water on each seed. It spreads fast through plain tissue (wide pipes) and slowly through the dark line (narrow pipes). So the two floods (the areas each seed's water has reached) meet near the dark line, where the full pipes of the bottleneck are. The floods are not how the cut is computed.     What can go wrong

    • The minimum cut only adds up the prices of the pixels it crosses. It has no notion of direction, so it will turn a right angle to follow something cheap, such as a tear. Section 8 fixes that.
    • The floods and the cut need not agree. On a section with a tear running most of the way across, the cut runs along the tear (cutting there is almost free), while the floods would not meet there.
    8

    A price on bending

    The boundary between two organoids does not turn sharp corners; a tear often does. So the cut is searched for a second time, now as a curve that pays an extra price for every turn.

    • The axis is the straight line from one seed to the other. Stations are lines parallel to the axis, one every 20 µm across the section. The curve crosses each station once; where it crosses is its position, measured along the axis in 5 µm steps.
    • The curve's price is the price of the pixels it crosses (the same price per µm as in section 6; nothing outside the tissue) plus a turn price of bend × (turn ÷ 90°)² at every station where it changes direction.
    • The program finds the cheapest such curve over the whole section exactly, by dynamic programming (part b). The pipeline uses bend = 1000 µm: a sharp 90° corner costs as much as cutting through 1,000 µm of plain tissue. bend = 0 gives back the minimum cut of section 7.

    Squaring the turn is what puts the price on sharp corners. One 90° corner costs bend; the same total turn made as three turns of 30° costs a third of that (figure on the right). An organoid's outline bends gently, while a tear or a crack turns sharply away from it.

    b · How the cheapest curve is found: dynamic programming

    Here a curve is simply one crossing point per station. A section has about a hundred stations with about a hundred possible positions at each. That gives more possible curves than there are atoms in the universe, far too many to price one by one. The search still finds the cheapest curve exactly, thanks to one fact: what the rest of a curve will cost depends only on its current position and its heading (the direction it is moving in), not on how it got there. So the search crosses the section once, station by station, and remembers only the cheapest way to arrive at each position with each heading.

    1. Lay out the frame. Draw the axis from the centre of the labelled seed to the centre of the other seed. The junction runs roughly across this axis. The stations are lines parallel to the axis, one every 20 µm across the whole section. The curve crosses each station at a single point, its position: the distance along the axis from the labelled seed, in 5 µm steps.
    2. Moves between stations. From one station to the next, the curve's position changes by at most 6 steps either way (30 µm sideways over 20 µm). That allows 13 possible headings, from straight across to about 56°.
    3. What a stretch costs. A stretch is the piece of curve between two neighbouring stations. Its cost is the price per µm under it (from section 6, read at its two ends and its middle) times its length. Crossing a seed is forbidden. Outside the tissue, crossing costs nothing, so the curve's ends meet the outline wherever that is cheapest.
    4. What a turn costs. Where the heading changes, the curve pays bend × (change ÷ 90°)².
    5. Fill a table, one station at a time. For every position and every heading at the current station, store one number: the lowest cost of any curve that starts at the first station and arrives here with this heading. To get it, compare the 13 ways to arrive, one for each heading at the previous station. Each way costs the number stored at the previous station, plus the turn, plus the stretch. Keep the cheapest, and note which of the 13 it came from. Only the previous station's table is needed, so the work grows with the number of stations, not with the number of possible curves.
    6. Walk back. At the last station, take the lowest number and follow the "came from" notes back to the first station. The result is the cheapest curve of all, exactly, not approximately. Each pixel is then given to the seed on its side of the curve. If the curve does not keep the two seeds apart, the minimum cut of section 7 is kept instead.

    The toy below does exactly this on a small grid: 18 stations (columns), 13 positions (rows) and 5 headings (straight, 45° and 63° either way). A faint junction runs straight across. A dark crack branches off it, down to where the tissue ends (dotted squares: outside the tissue, free to cross). Step through the stations with the player: each square shows the lowest cost of reaching it, and blue lines show which square of the previous station that cost came from. Then change the price on bending with the slider.

    What can go wrong

    • Squaring the turn makes one sharp corner expensive but the same turn spread along the curve cheap, so the curve can still bend round an organoid. A tear that runs roughly in the same direction as the junction needs no sharp turn, so it can still win.
    • The failed section (switch the sample above). The labelled organoid has two dense patches of AF488, one at each end. The labelled seed covers only the larger patch, in one corner (section 5). So the cut only has to cut off that corner. Open tears running from the junction to the lower edge let it do so cheaply: a tear is empty, so it is the cheapest thing to cut, while the true junction is faint there. The tear saves more than its turns cost, so the price on bending does not pull the line back onto the junction (341 µm off). With a seed that also covers the second patch, the line lands 17 µm from the hand-drawn one. The status is still ok: a line along a tear looks dark and certain, so only a look at the picture (section 10) catches the error.
    • The value 1000 µm was chosen by comparison with hand-drawn junctions. Compared with 400 µm, it keeps the typical (median) error the same and brings the worst samples closer. It was not tuned further.
    9

    From the cut to a line, from the line to a band

    The cut follows pixel edges, so it zigzags by a few pixels; a real organoid boundary does not. So the cut is turned into a smooth junction line, and the line is used to mark the band: the strip of tissue just past the junction in which cells are counted.

    • Line: take the pixels where the two halves touch, thin them to a curve one pixel wide, and order its points from one end to the other. Then smooth it with a running mean over 150 µm and keep one point every 40 µm.
    • Band: all tissue of one organoid (by default the one the labelled cells migrate into) whose distance from the line lies between the gap and gap + width. The gap (2 µm) keeps the line itself out of the band.
    • Rim: the band also reaches 10 µm past the tissue's edge. This catches cells migrating over the organoid's surface, just outside the outline the stain gives. The rim never reaches into the other organoid.

    Your browser computes the band below from the line, using each pixel's distance from the line, its side, the tissue mask and the rim. Change the width, the side or the rim with the controls. The original Fiji macro instead shifted the line sideways to make a strip (show the strip). A strip ends in two straight edges wherever the line stops; the band ends where the organoid ends.

    What can go wrong

    • A line shorter than the junction (for example, one that stops early at a ragged end) gives a band that covers only part of the junction. Dividing a count by the length of that short line inflates the count "per mm" (section 14).
    • The band is flat (2D) and built on the overview. To use it at full resolution, the program fills its smooth outline on the full-resolution grid, rather than enlarging the overview pixels.

    • The rule was found on the same 30 samples it is judged on (5 of them Damaged). It still has to be confirmed on the next batch.
    11

    Finding the cells

    From here on the work is at full resolution. Cellpose (a trained neural network) outlines each nucleus in the nuclear stain. AF488 fills more of the cell than the nucleus, so each nucleus is grown by 3 µm. Where two growing neighbours meet, each pixel goes to the nearer nucleus, so they share the gap. The ring, from 3 to 6 µm outside the nucleus, measures the cell's own local background.

    Choose the picture and tick the layers to show. Move the mouse over a cell to read its numbers; click to keep that cell selected.

    What can go wrong

    • Two touching nuclei can be merged into one, or a faint nucleus can be missed. 87 % of the cells clicked by hand had a nucleus found by Cellpose under them.
    • A cell tightly packed among its neighbours has no ring, because every pixel 3–6 µm out belongs to another cell. For such a cell, the background is the channel's local median over 45 µm instead.
    12

    Calling a cell positive

    Every cell now has a brightness (its mean AF488 over the grown nucleus). Which cells carry the label? AF488 is dim and patchy, and there is no clear gap between negative and positive cells. So a cell is called positive when it is brighter than a threshold, and the choice of threshold is the biggest single decision in the count. Four kinds of rule were tried:

    • median + k · SD of the band's cells. The SD here is the robust SD (1.4826 × the median distance from the median), so a few bright cells do not inflate it. The current junction analysis uses k = 3 on the raw channel.
    • The same rule on the median-filtered channel: each cell's value minus the 45 µm local median (section 4), which removes slow haze.
    • Ring contrast: the cell is at least r times as bright as its own ring.
    • Negative-control percentile: the cell is brighter than 99.9 % of the negative control cells. These lie deep in the organoid where AF488 is not at home, that is, not the organoid the labelled cells started in: more than 300 µm past the line and more than 30 µm inside the tissue. Few of them should carry the label.

    Choose a rule and move its slider, or drag the threshold on the histogram. The histogram, the map and the counts update. The cells clicked by hand (a second opinion, not the truth) give a live precision and recall: how many of the cells called positive were clicked, and how many of the clicked cells were called positive.

    Why "median + 3 SD" overcounts, and why "median + 7 SD, median-filtered" was chosen

    Most of the band's cells are negative, but their brightness has a long tail of bright values from haze, cells at the tissue edge and autofluorescence. With a robust SD, a threshold at 3 SD lets through a few % of any such tail. So cells are called positive where there are no migrants, and the threshold shifts with whatever the band happens to cover.     What can go wrong

    • No rule separates the cells cleanly. Even the best rule calls about 2 % of the cells on the home side (the organoid the labelled cells started in) positive, and 0.4 % just past the line. The clicks agree with it at F1 0.38; noisy clicks limit this agreement as much as the rule does.
    • Percentile rules do not give the false-positive rate they promise. Staining falls off with distance, so a threshold taken from one part of the control does not hold in another part. (The 99th percentile gave 2.1 % false positives when the threshold was set on one part of the control and tested on another.)
    • Rules that look for two populations fail: AF488 over a section does not have two separate humps (Otsu's method calls half the cells positive).
    13

    Cells that crossed the line

    Each label has a home side: the side of the junction line where its organoid is. A positive cell found on the other side, where its label is not at home, has crossed: it migrated from its own organoid into the other one.

    • The home side is read from the data, separately for each sample and channel. It is the side whose brightest cells (95th percentile of ring contrast, within 300 µm of the line) stand out more. AF488 and AF555 came out at home on opposite sides in 28 of 30 samples.
    • Every cell gets a signed distance to the line: positive into the organoid where AF488 is not at home, negative on its home side. Cells beyond either end of the line are on neither side and are left out.
    • A cell has crossed if it is positive, lies more than a small gap (2 µm) past the line, and lies within a chosen distance: 50, 100 or 300 µm, or any distance. Dividing the number of crossed cells by the length of the line gives crossed per mm of junction.

    Which cells are positive follows the rule set in section 12; change it there. Here, choose how far past the line to count. Teal dots are positive cells on the home side, yellow dots are crossed cells; the dashed line marks the chosen distance.

    What can go wrong

    • Counts within the first 50 µm depend on exactly where the line is drawn. With two people's hand-drawn lines, the counts within 300 µm agree far better (CCC 0.97) than the counts within 50 µm (38 % relative difference).
    • The negative control lies in the same area where far-travelling migrants are counted. Under the negative-control rules, a sample with many far-travelling cells therefore raises its own threshold.
    14

    Counting fairly, and what came out

    A raw count depends on how big the section is. There are three ways to make samples comparable, each answering a slightly different question:

    • % positive: positive cells ÷ all cells in the band. It does not depend on the size of the section, but it does depend on how many cells the band holds.
    • per mm of junction: positive cells ÷ length of the line. It answers "how many cells cross per length of contact?".
    • per mm² of band: positive cells ÷ band area.

    The tiles below use the band and the rule from section 12 and update with them. Move the slider to shorten the line, as the finder sometimes does: the count per mm rises although nothing about the cells has changed.

    Slides, not sections, are the unit

    One slide carries several sections, and the sections on a slide may come from one assembloid. So they are not independent of each other. Every genotype test is therefore run twice: once on single samples, and once on slide means (one mean per slide: 8 WT3 slides against 11 GD1). The test on slides is the honest one. Its p-values are larger because it has fewer data points.
